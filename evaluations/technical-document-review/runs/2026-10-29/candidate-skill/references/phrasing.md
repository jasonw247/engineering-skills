# Phrasing and organization examples

Use this reference when repeated abstractions, compressed prose, or formulaic language obstruct comprehension. Examples illustrate editorial choices; they are not required replacements.

## Diagnose the meaning before rewriting

For a difficult passage, identify the subject, action, relationship, and consequence for the reader. When a sentence supports several meanings, inspect surrounding material or its source. If the ambiguity remains, report it instead of choosing an interpretation merely to improve fluency.

| Passage | Possible revision and condition |
| --- | --- |
| “Maintain scoped evidence lineage.” | If this is the intended requirement: “Record the source ID, version, and passage location for each claim.” |
| “A computed narrative needs its population definition.” | “When an answer reports a calculation, identify the records included and provide the computed result.” |
| “Only compatible reuse contributes; frequent edits shorten that horizon.” | “Count savings only while a saved result remains valid. Frequent source changes reduce opportunities for reuse.” |
| “Costs may fall if volume increases.” | “Costs may fall with higher volume.” Preserve “may”; the source has not established a guaranteed reduction. |
| “The hierarchy enables robust cross-document intelligence.” | Identify what it actually does, such as routing a query to related documents, then explain the supported benefit and its conditions. Do not invent a benefit. |

Split independent requirements into a list when useful. For a causal explanation, use connected prose that explains why one step follows another. Merely dividing a long sentence can leave the missing relationship unexplained.

## Treat patterns as signals

Repeated rhetorical questions, emphatic contrasts, promotional adjectives, symmetrical lists, and abstract headings can obscure meaning. Inspect them in context. Retain a contrast when it prevents a real misunderstanding, a technical term when it is precise, and passive voice when the actor is unknown or unimportant.

Do not infer authorship from “LLM-like” phrasing. Avoid mechanical substitutions, arbitrary sentence-length limits, or removing every occurrence of a word such as “robust.” A robust statistical estimator is legitimate terminology.

## Match organization to the reader's task

Useful shapes, not mandatory section templates:

- Research review: question and scope, mechanisms and evidence, comparisons, conclusions and open questions.
- Design guide: baseline, diagnosis, implementation choices, validation, operation.
- Procedure: prerequisites, actions, expected results, exception handling.
- Proposal: problem, proposed change, evidence and alternatives, costs and decision.
- Reference: concepts or interfaces organized for lookup.

Assume competence in the reader's field without assuming expertise in every adjacent specialty. Introduce an unfamiliar mechanism with what changes, why it may help, and what it costs. A list of model or library names rarely supplies that explanation.
